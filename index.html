Spot the Misleading Chart

Every number in these six charts is real. Each chart is still drawn to give you the wrong idea. Find the trick, then see the same numbers drawn honestly.

What makes this chart misleading?

A checklist for any chart

  1. Where does the axis start? Bars should start at zero. If they don’t, small differences look huge.
  2. Which years are shown, and which are missing? Ask what the line looks like before the first point and between the points.
  3. Are the gaps between points equal? Check the labels along the bottom, not just the spacing.
  4. Which way is up? Read the axis numbers before you read the shape.
  5. Is size doing the talking? When pictures or circles stand in for numbers, compare the numbers yourself.
  6. Are the groups really comparable? Populations differ by age, size, and time. A fair comparison adjusts for that, or uses rates in place of counts.
  7. Who made it, and what do they want you to conclude? The headline tells you what the chart’s maker hopes you’ll see.

Why it works on us

We read shapes faster than numbers. A steep line or a tall bar registers before the axis labels do. Chart makers who want a reaction rely on that.

None of these tricks require lying. That’s what makes them useful to advertisers, campaigns, and advocates of every political stripe. Darrell Huff catalogued them in How to Lie with Statistics in 1954, and they haven’t changed much.

Not every unusual choice is dishonest. A line chart of temperatures or stock prices doesn’t need to start at zero, and a chart of rankings puts number 1 at the top. The question is whether the design helps you see what the numbers say or pushes you toward something they don’t.

Talk about it

  1. Which chart fooled you, or came closest? What did your eye go to first?
  2. Find a chart in the news or on social media this week. Run it through the checklist. Does it pass?
  3. Take one of the honest charts here and write two different headlines for it, one alarming and one reassuring. Is either one a lie?
  4. Statistician Edward Tufte proposed a “lie factor”: the size of the effect shown in the picture divided by the size of the effect in the data. Roughly what’s the lie factor for the circle chart?

About the data

Life expectancy and death rates: National Center for Health Statistics, “Mortality in the United States, 2024” and “Leading Causes of Death, 1900–1998” (the 2024 cancer rate is deaths divided by population). Violent victimization: Bureau of Justice Statistics, Criminal Victimization, 2024, appendix table 1 (2006 is omitted because BJS says it isn’t comparable). Crime perceptions: Gallup’s annual Crime survey. Wealth shares: Federal Reserve, Distributional Financial Accounts, second quarter of 2026.

Further reading: Huff, Darrell. 1954. How to Lie with Statistics. Norton. Tufte, Edward R. 1983. The Visual Display of Quantitative Information. Graphics Press. Cairo, Alberto. 2019. How Charts Lie. Norton.
